## BotDojo: AI-Powered Research Agent

Unifying customer and product insights that were trapped in Gong, Salesforce, Zendesk, and Productboard into a single, queryable surface so teams could respond faster with confidence.

**Tags:** AI Research, Insight Aggregation, Connector Platform

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Role** | Research systems lead |
| **Methods** | Connector audits, synthesis automation, contextual inquiry |
| **Users** | PMs, UX researchers, customer-facing teams |
| **Outcome** | Always-on AI agent that surfaces insight bundles across four platforms |

### Phase 1: Problem Definition & Opportunity

#### Context

Across Mitratech's GRC business unit, insights lived in Gong conversation notes, Salesforce CRM data, Zendesk tickets, and Productboard feedback, yet no single pathway made those signals usable. Teams worked with stale, fragmented information.

#### How I Identified the Problem

- Observed research and product workflows that relied on manual synthesis.
- Stakeholder discussions noted repeated delays in getting signal clarity.
- Decision patterns relied on partial data or outdated assumptions.
- Direct research showed insight gathering was slow and inconsistent.

#### Key Problems

- Insights were fragmented across tools, requiring manual effort to compile.
- Research synthesis was time-intensive and not scalable.
- Teams lacked real-time access to user signals.
- Decisions were often based on incomplete or outdated data.

#### Voices from the Team: Fragmented Research Pain Points

> “There’s no shared source of truth. Everyone has a piece of the puzzle, but no one sees the whole picture.”

> 
> Product Lead

> “I know the insights exist somewhere, but pulling them together takes more time than the decision itself.”

> 
> Product Manager

> “We end up relying on the last thing we remember rather than the full picture, because getting that picture is just too slow.”

> 
> Senior Product Manager

> “Every time we start a new initiative, it feels like we’re rediscovering the same insights instead of building on existing knowledge.”

> 
> Product Manager

> “There’s data in Gong, feedback in Zendesk, notes in Productboard… but no single place to actually make sense of it.”

> 
> Product Manager

> “Half of my time goes into finding and stitching insights together, not actually understanding the user.”

> 
> Design Researcher

> “We’re not lacking data. We’re lacking a way to connect it meaningfully.”

> 
> UX Researcher

> “Insights are scattered, and by the time we compile them, they’re already outdated.”

> 
> Design Researcher

> “We do good research, but it doesn’t scale because every project starts from scratch.”

> 
> UX Designer

#### Why This Needed to Be Solved

Delays hurt research and design cycles, increased reliance on individuals, reduced confidence, and blocked scaling research across products. The opportunity was to shift from periodic research to continuous, accessible insight.

### Phase 2: Solution Design

#### Strategy

Built a centralized, AI-powered system that aggregates multiple sources, enables natural-language querying, and scales research accessibility.

#### Solution Overview

Designed and launched BotDojo, an AI research agent that normalizes data, surfaces summaries, and links back to source artifacts.

#### Core System Design

- Data inputs: Gong, Salesforce, Zendesk, Productboard.
- AI layer: aggregation, processing, and synthesis of signals.
- Interface: queryable surface with contextual summaries.

#### Key Design Decisions

- Centralized fragmented data without replacing existing tools.
- Enabled natural querying to reduce manual filtering.
- Prioritized fast, usable insights over perfect analysis.
- Designed for cross-functional use across design, PM, and customer teams.

#### Stakeholder Alignment

Positioned the system as a way to reduce friction, showed faster insight retrieval, and secured engineering agreement on connector feasibility.

### Phase 3: Implementation & Adoption

#### Implementation

- Built BotDojo with connectors to the four enterprise tools.
- Designed a simple interface for querying insights and reviewing sources.
- Ensured outputs were actionable and surfaced clear next steps.

#### Driving Adoption

- Embedded BotDojo into existing workflows rather than building new rituals.
- Demonstrated use cases inside design and product scenarios.
- Enabled teams with self-serve access and reduced dependency on centralized researchers.

The principle was to make the system easier to use than not to use.

### Impact

- 50% reduction in research cycle time (3 weeks → 1.5 weeks).
- Real-time insight access across PM, design, and customer support teams.
- Less dependency on manual synthesis and faster decision making.
- Scalable research availability across multiple product teams.

### Reflection

The biggest challenge was not the lack of data but the lack of accessibility and synthesis; treating research as a system allowed broader adoption. AI enabled the shift, but the real value was in embedding it meaningfully into workflows.

## AI Search Across Risk, Third-Party, and Policy Tools

Designed a unified, intelligent search experience across risk, governance, compliance, controls, and questionnaire modules so Enterprise Risk Manager users can find answers without hopping between silos.

**Tags:** Enterprise AI, Risk & Compliance, Governance

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Role** | Design lead, global search experience |
| **Methods** | User interviews, workflow observation, intent mapping |
| **Users** | Risk managers, compliance operators, governance leads |
| **Outcome** | AI-powered global search across siloed GRC modules |

### Phase 1: Problem Definition & Opportunity

#### Context

Enterprise Risk Manager, one of 13 products in Mitratech's GRC portfolio, bundled risk, governance, compliance, controls, and questionnaire modules, yet each function lived in a separate silo. Critical information existed, but users had to navigate across modules manually, slowing down decision-making.

The search experience was inconsistent: accuracy varied per module, discoverability suffered, and teams often bypassed search entirely.

- Risk
- Governance
- Compliance
- Controls
- Questionnaires

#### How I Identified the Problem

- User interviews revealed frustration with cross-module navigation.
- Support tickets highlighted recurring search accuracy complaints.
- Productboard feedback contained repeated asks for better cross-module visibility.
- Workflow observation showed users favoring manual jumps over search.

#### User Interview Insights

- “If I’m reviewing a risk, I usually need related controls or policies… but I have to jump between sections to piece it together.” _(Risk Analyst)_

- “Search exists, but I don’t rely on it. It doesn’t always return what I expect.” _(Compliance Manager)_

- “Finding the right information depends on knowing exactly where it lives.” _(Governance Lead)_

- “I spend more time navigating than actually analyzing the data.” _(Risk Manager)_

- “Even when I search, I get too many results that aren’t relevant. It’s faster to just click through.” _(Internal Auditor)_

- “We deal with controls and questionnaires a lot, but there’s no easy way to connect them to risks without digging around.” _(Third-Party Risk Specialist)_

- “The lack of contextual results makes me double-check every search outcome.” _(Security Lead)_

- “I need a single answer, not a list of unrelated records.” _(Audit Director)_

- “Connecting risks with controls should happen automatically; currently it feels manual.” _(Control Owner)_

#### Key Problems Identified

- No unified search across the five core modules.
- Users relied on manual navigation across sections.
- Existing search behaved inconsistently and missed context.
- Heavy cognitive load due to data-dense interfaces.
- Difficulty connecting related information spread across modules.

#### Why This Needed to Be Solved

This inefficiency slowed information access, weakened decision-making, and frustrated users in a high-stakes environment. The opportunity was to transition from navigation-driven workflows to search-driven access.

### Phase 2: Solution Design

#### Strategy

Designed a global, AI-powered search system that operates across every module, learns user intent, and surfaces relevant results without forcing module switches.

#### Solution Overview

Introduced a single unified search interface for Enterprise Risk Manager that indexes risk records, governance structures, compliance data, controls, and questionnaires.

#### Key Design Decisions

##### 1. Unified Search Entry Point

- Created a prominent search bar as the primary entry point.
- Ensured users no longer needed to switch modules manually.
- Delivered consistent behavior across data types.

##### 2. Context-Aware Results

- Interpret intent beyond keywords.
- Prioritize results by relevance and contextual cues.
- Group answers by module (risk, governance, compliance, controls, questionnaires).

##### 3. Scalable Information Architecture

- Handled large volumes of enterprise data with grouping and hierarchy.
- Prepared the system to scale into future modules.

##### 4. AI-Driven Enhancements

- Used AI-assisted ranking for partial, ambiguous, and natural-language queries.
- Reduced noise, surfaced signal quickly.

##### 5. Interaction Design

- Optimized for keyboard-first interactions.
- Allowed progressive refinement of results.
- Reduced the steps needed to reach critical records.

#### Stakeholder Alignment

Partnered with Product and Engineering to balance relevance with performance, demonstrated workflow wins, and positioned global search as a strategic capability.

## Law Firm Billing Acceleration

Reducing billing cycle time from 20 days to 5 by unifying onboarding, rate approvals, and billing into a single guided workflow.

**Tags:** Legal Tech, Revenue Ops, Workflow Design

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Role** | Lead Product Designer & Workflow Architect |
| **Methods** | Process mapping, collaboration workshops, prototyping |
| **Users** | Timekeepers, Billing Managers, Finance Leaders |
| **Outcome** | Billing cycle cut from 20 days to 5 |

### Phase 1: Problem Definition & Opportunity

#### Context

Across Mitratech's legal operations products, billing workflows were fragmented over multiple systems; onboarding, rate approvals, and billing ran independently with separate inputs, reviews, and approvals. The fragmentation created delays, redundant work, and low visibility for finance and legal ops.

#### How I Identified the Problem

- Process mapping sessions across the full billing lifecycle.
- Cross-functional workshops with timekeepers, billing managers, and finance.
- Observed system-hopping and repeated handoffs in real workflows.
- Time-loss analysis across onboarding, approvals, and billing stages.

The issue was a chain of disconnected steps, not a single bottleneck.

#### Key Problems Identified

- Onboarding, approvals, and billing lived in separate systems with no continuity.
- Multiple handoffs caused redundant reviews and delays.
- Finance lacked real-time visibility into progress.
- Manual coordination (emails, follow-ups) was required to move work forward.
- Errors and missing data surfaced late, causing rework.

#### Voices from the Workflow

- “I approve rates in one system, then have to chase the same info again when billing starts.” _(Billing Manager)_

- “We lose days waiting for onboarding data to be re-entered for finance.” _(Timekeeper Lead)_

- “Finance can’t see what’s blocked until someone emails us; we’re always reacting late.” _(Finance Operations)_

- “Approvals happen, but billing doesn’t know; so we re-review the same thing.” _(Legal Ops)_

- “If a required document is missing, we find out at the end, not the start.” _(Billing Analyst)_

- “Most of my time is spent coordinating handoffs, not moving invoices.” _(Billing Coordinator)_

#### Why This Needed to Be Solved

Billing delays hurt cash flow, increased manual workload, and eroded confidence in timelines. Teams spent more time coordinating than completing work. The opportunity: shift from a fragmented, multi-step process to a unified, guided workflow.

### Phase 2: Solution Design

#### Strategy

Design a single, continuous workflow that removes handoffs, reduces duplication, provides cross-role visibility, and embeds validation early.

#### Solution Overview

A guided billing workflow combining timekeeper onboarding, rate documentation/approvals, and billing execution into one connected experience.

#### Key Design Interventions

##### 1. Unified Workflow Experience

- Consolidated steps into one guided flow from onboarding → approvals → billing.
- Eliminated system switching and reduced context shifts.

##### 2. Persona-Based Workflow Cards

- Connected cards for Timekeepers, Reviewers, and Finance teams.
- Each card surfaces relevant actions, dependencies, and ownership.

##### 3. Embedded Compliance & Validation

- Compliance guardrails and AI-assisted validation early in the flow.
- Prevents bad data from reaching approval stages.

##### 4. Approval Integration into Billing Queue

- Approvals live inside the billing workflow, removing duplicate reviews.
- Shorter wait times between steps.

##### 5. Finance Visibility Dashboard

- Dashboards to monitor progress, bottlenecks, and pending approvals.
- Removes reliance on email follow-ups.

#### Stakeholder Alignment

Workshops aligned timekeepers (ease), legal ops (compliance), and finance (visibility). Demonstrated value via simplified flows and reduced delays.

### Phase 3: Implementation & Adoption

#### Implementation

- Rolled out the unified workflow incrementally to limit disruption.
- Validated with prototypes and stakeholder feedback.
- Integrated with existing systems and processes.

#### Driving Adoption

- Made the unified flow easier than legacy methods.
- Delivered real-time visibility to finance, building trust.
- Reinforced with alignment sessions and feedback loops.

### Impact

- Billing cycle reduced from 20 days to 5.
- Redundant approvals and manual coordination eliminated.
- Improved cash flow and revenue realization.
- Lower operational load; proactive exception handling.

### Reflection

Workflow pain came from system fragmentation, not user behavior. Connecting steps, reducing handoffs, and giving visibility delivered the biggest gains. Designing for the whole workflow, not single screens, drove speed and efficiency.

## Policy Document Summaries

Enabling legal teams to quickly understand policy changes and take action through AI-generated briefings embedded within the platform.

**Tags:** Policy AI, Legal Ops, Knowledge Workflows

> **Note:** This project is in progress; full case study details will be available soon. Happy to walk you through the current work. Email [sujithanand96@gmail.com](mailto:sujithanand96@gmail.com).

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Role** | Head of design for the GRC business unit |
| **Methods** | Document audits, prompt engineering, workflow testing |
| **Users** | Legal teams, compliance officers, policy reviewers |
| **Focus** | AI integration, decision support, workflow efficiency |

### Phase 1: Problem Definition & Opportunity

#### Context

In Mitratech's policy management product, legal and compliance teams routinely review lengthy, dense policy documents to spot changes, assess impact, and trigger actions. Parallel updates increased effort and risk of delay.

#### How I Identified the Problem

- Observed users working inside the policy platform, watching how they navigated documents.
- Interviews with legal and compliance teams about review workflows and delays.
- Noted repeated manual scanning to extract key points and implications.
- Mapped where comprehension, not access, was the bottleneck.

#### Key Problems Identified

- Reviews were time-consuming; key changes had to be found manually.
- Critical updates could be missed or acted on late.
- No structured way to surface follow-ups or owners.
- Review quality and speed varied across users.

#### Why This Needed to Be Solved

Slow decision-making increased risk, raised cognitive load, and hurt efficiency when multiple updates landed together. The shift needed was from reading documents to understanding and acting on them.

### Phase 2: Solution Design

#### Strategy

Augment documents with intelligence so users can understand key updates quickly, know what matters, and act confidently without leaving their workflow.

#### Solution Overview

Designed an AI-powered briefing system embedded in the policy platform that generates concise summaries, highlights critical changes, and surfaces follow-up actions.

#### Key Design Interventions

##### 1. AI-Generated Summaries

- Extract key information from long-form policies into structured, readable briefs.
- Cut the time to grasp updates.

##### 2. Highlighting Critical Changes

- Flag important updates and point to the relevant sections.
- Reduce risk of missing critical information.

##### 3. Action-Oriented Briefings

- Turn summaries into actionable insights with suggested follow-ups.

## Your Resume Is Your First Design Deliverable

Sujith Anand Head of Design, GRC October 31, 2025

I recently opened a designer role on my team and received thousands of resumes. More than 95% didn’t make it past the first stage. For Product, UI, and UX designers, your evaluation begins with your resume. It is your first design deliverable, even before your portfolio.

### Common Mistakes Designers Should Fix

Spelling and grammar errors

Mistake: Typos, incorrect grammar, or inconsistent phrasing across your resume. Why it’s a red flag: It shows poor attention to detail, which is essential for any design role. Recommendation: Proofread carefully, use spelling tools, and ask another designer to review it.

Misaligned content or inconsistent spacing

Mistake: Uneven alignment, irregular margins, or unpredictable spacing between sections. Why it’s a red flag: It suggests weak visual fundamentals and distracts from your story. Recommendation: Use grids or layout guides for visual balance, and test readability across different screens.

Low-quality images or poor font choices

Mistake: Using pixelated headshots or decorative fonts that reduce clarity. Why it’s a red flag: It undermines your credibility as a visual designer. Recommendation: Use a clean, professional photo and legible, modern fonts. With modern AI tools like Nano Banana, it takes just a few seconds to create a polished, professional headshot. Always preview your resume visually before sending it out.

Listing only tasks, not outcomes

Mistake: Focusing on what you did (“designed screens,” “updated icons”) without explaining the results. Why it’s a red flag: It misses the opportunity to demonstrate impact and value. Recommendation: Highlight accomplishments and quantify results where possible. Show how your design work improved retention, conversion, or engagement.

Treating your resume as just a document

Mistake: Seeing it as paperwork instead of a reflection of your design thinking. Why it’s a red flag: Hiring managers assess clarity, hierarchy, and communication through your resume design. Recommendation: Approach it like any design project. Make it clear, balanced, and intentional in showing the value you bring.

Your design journey starts with your resume. Make it the first piece that truly reflects your craft.

## Does Good Design Even Need to Be Seen?

Sujith Anand Head of Design, GRC February 15, 2025

I came across a post that said:

"Good design is like salt: you don’t notice it when it’s there, but you realize its value when it’s missing."

That stuck with me.

As designers, we focus on details, color palettes, typography, and animations. We want our work to stand out. But the real power of design is in how well it blends in.

Think about the last time you used a great product. Did you stop to admire how intuitive it was? Probably not. But I bet you noticed when something was frustrating, when a website was confusing, or when an app made a simple task harder than it needed to be.

That’s the paradox of good design. It’s invisible when done right and painfully obvious when done wrong.

### My Own Experience with Invisible Design

Early in my career, I believed a good design should impress. If it looked beautiful, it was successful. But over time, I realized something: users don’t care about how "designed" something looks. They care about how it works.

I once worked on a project where we introduced a sleek, modern UI refresh. Internally, everyone loved it. It looked clean, polished, and sophisticated. But when it reached actual users, we got a very different response. They found it overwhelming. Things that were once easy to find were now buried under layers of "aesthetic improvements."

That was a hard lesson. We had focused so much on making the design that we forgot the real goal of making it usable.

### What I Learned About Invisible Design

- Good design gets out of the way. If users can complete tasks effortlessly without noticing the design, it’s working.
- Users don’t want to "learn" a design. Every extra second spent figuring out an interface is friction.
- Simplicity wins. More details don’t make a design better. It’s better when there are fewer distractions.
- Success isn’t in visual praise but in usability. If no one talks about your design because it just works, that’s a win.

Early in my career, I thought a successful design was one that impressed people. But I’ve learned to ask a different question: Does this feel effortless?

So, does good design even need to be seen? Or is its success measured by how little people think about it?

## From Perfectionism to Progress: Embracing Imperfection in Design

Sujith Anand Head of Design, GRC January 17, 2025

**Tags:** MVD, Iteration, AI-assisted editing

Like most designers, I used to suffer from the disease of making "THE PERFECT DESIGN." I truly believed I could make it happen that every pixel and interaction could be flawless. I spent countless hours perfecting every detail, convinced it was the right path. When colleagues and peers said, "This looks perfect," I became determined to keep proving I could design the perfect solution. But the reality? It was different.

I learned this lesson the hard way. I worked on a project where I spent days refining the design. Internally, everyone loved it, but when the design was shown to the end users, their reaction was nothing like I expected. They found it overwhelming and confusing. It was a crushing disappointment.

I had to step back and rethink my approach. This time, I carried a piece of advice from my previous boss: “Perfection is the enemy of getting started.” And that’s when it clicked. All that time spent perfecting the design had slowed us down and failed to deliver real value. Sometimes, all we need is a Minimum Viable Design (MVD).

Here’s what changed after that experience:

Move quickly, and test early. A rough but functional version often does more good than something overly polished and disconnected from reality.

Feedback drives improvement. Get designs into users' hands fast to see what works and what doesn’t before diving into perfection.

Embrace imperfections. Good design isn’t about being flawless. It’s about learning, adjusting, and improving based on real-world feedback.

These days, I’m far quicker to get designs out into the wild. I focus less on making things "perfect" and more on learning and improving with each iteration. Good design comes from embracing imperfections, not locking yourself away to get everything "just right."

## The Art of Saying No: When Designs Speak for Themselves

Sujith Anand Head of Design, GRC January 11, 2025

**Tags:** Prioritization, UX Focus, AI-assisted editing

As designers, we are no strangers to requests.

“Can we add one more feature here?”

“Let’s make that button bigger.”

“Maybe another icon would not hurt?”

At first glance, these ideas might seem harmless, even helpful. But when you stack them all together, the result is rarely good. My previous boss often emphasized the need for "ruthless prioritization," and that phrase has stayed with me ever since. It is about making tough choices to focus on what truly matters instead of giving in to every suggestion that comes along.

I often said yes to these requests early on, thinking it made me a better collaborator. It did not take long to realize that too many yeses dilute the very thing we are here to do, which is create meaningful, focused user experiences.

One project still stands out. We were tasked with updating an interface for a legacy system. It had all the charm you would expect from older tech, but none. The requests kept piling in, and slowly the screen turned into a visual puzzle for users to navigate. Sure, the system technically worked, but was it simple? Efficient? A pleasure to use? Absolutely not.

That is when I realized every design element has to justify its presence. If it does not improve the user experience, it is just clutter. And that is not just true for design, it applies to a lot in life.

Saying no is not about rejecting ideas outright. It is about making intentional choices to preserve clarity and focus. It is about trusting that a well-designed experience can often speak louder without unnecessary extras.

Now, before agreeing to a new design element, I ask myself:

Will this help the user achieve their goal faster or easier?

Does it simplify the flow or complicate things?

Is it adding value or just adding noise?

Design is not about stuffing everything in. It is about creating the space for what truly matters. Sometimes the most powerful design decisions are the ones that do not shout but simply speak for themselves.

What is your approach to prioritization in design or work?
